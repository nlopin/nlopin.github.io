One idea, twice · version A

A cubic Bézier curve

B(t) = (1−t)3P0 + 3(1−t)2t P1 + 3(1−t)t2P2 + t3P3,  t ∈ [0, 1]

A cubic Bézier curve is a parametric curve defined by four control points P0…P3. The curve passes through P0 and P3; P1 and P2 determine the tangent directions at the endpoints. Each point of the curve is a convex combination of the control points, weighted by the Bernstein polynomials of degree 3. Equivalently, the curve can be evaluated by repeated linear interpolation (de Casteljau's algorithm).

~0:02. Read it out loud, at normal speed, once. Don't explain. Ask: hands up if you could now draw the curve for four points I give you. Everyone here has used a Bézier curve (cubic-bezier() in CSS, the pen tool in any design app). Keep it to 40 seconds.

~0:05. Victor's essay coined the term. A printed page lets the reader read; it doesn't let them test the author's claim. His examples: a budget proposal where you drag the tax rate in the sentence and the deficit in the next sentence changes; a filter-design article with a working filter in it. Today's token bucket is an explorable example. The essay is at worrydream.com/ExplorableExplanations.

Nicky Case · How I Make Explorable Explanations

Three steps

1

Start with a question

“Make them love your question” before you answer it.

2

Climb the ladder

Start on the ground with one concrete case; move up step by step to the general rule.

3

End with a question

A sandbox where the reader asks their own. It “allows the student to go beyond the teacher”.

Case made Parable of the Polygons (with Vi Hart), The Evolution of Trust, We Become What We Behold, and runs the explorabl.es hub.

~0:06. The post is short (blog.ncase.me/how-i-make-an-explorable-explanation); it's on the reading list. The next three slides take one step each. Case also says the goal is to “smart the people up”, not to “dumb the ideas down”: the ladder is how you get a non-expert to the real idea.

Step 1 · two openings for the same game

Which one would you keep reading?

A

The iterated prisoner's dilemma is a repeated two-player game in which each player chooses to cooperate or defect. Payoffs: mutual cooperation 2, mutual defection 0, defection against cooperation 3 / −1.

B

During World War I, peace broke out. It was Christmas 1914 on the Western Front, and British and German soldiers left their trenches to share food and sing carols. Why did enemies trust each other, and why don't friends?

B is how The Evolution of Trust opens. The definition in A arrives later, once you want it.

~0:07. B is a close paraphrase of Trust's first screen, not a quote. A is accurate and answers a question nobody has asked yet. Parable of the Polygons goes further: you're moving unhappy shapes within ten seconds and only find out afterwards that it's about segregation. The question doesn't have to be stated as a question; it has to make the reader want the answer.

“Start concrete, start grounded.” — Victor, Learnable Programming

~0:09. Victor's essay walks a car-steering algorithm from one run, to a slider over a parameter, to a plot over all values of the parameter. Readers who start at the top (the rule) can repeat it but can't use it. Readers who start at the bottom can see why it's true. The token bucket demo's steps follow exactly these three rungs.

Matt Stone & Trey Parker, via Nicky Case

“And then” is a list. “But” and “therefore” are a story.

As written first

  1. A token bucket holds tokens, and then
  2. each request spends one, and then
  3. tokens refill at a fixed rate, and then
  4. there's a capacity, and then
  5. bursts are limited, and then
  6. here's a sandbox.

Rewritten

  1. Six requests arrive at once. Four pass.
  2. Therefore each request spends a token.
  3. But later requests pass again,
  4. therefore tokens come back over time.
  5. But after a long wait, the same burst loses two again,
  6. therefore capacity caps the burst. Your turn: …

~0:11. The South Park writers' rule (from a 2011 NYU class): if the beats of your outline join with "and then", you have a sequence of events; join them with "but" or "therefore" and each beat is caused by the one before. Case uses it to order the sections of Trust. Show the left column; ask the room which beat could become a "but". Then reveal the right. Each "but" is a surprise the reader can see in the figure; each "therefore" is the rule that explains it. The right column is the step order of the token-bucket demo; a test (test/claims.test.mjs) checks that the steps' first words still read Four / But / Therefore / But / Therefore. The full outline is in demos/but-therefore.md.

Step 3

End with a sandbox, or a question

  • Trust ends with a sandbox: change the population, the payoffs, the noise. Test claims the author never made.
  • The token bucket ends with: twelve evenly spaced requests, a bucket of 3: what is the slowest refill rate that lets all twelve through? Now drag four to the same moment.
  • The sandbox comes last. Given first, it's a dashboard with no starting point.

~0:12. The ending question should be answerable with the controls the reader now has, and shouldn't have been answered in the text. The token-bucket answers: 1.05 tokens per second on the slider for the evenly spaced twelve, exactly 9/8.8 ≈ 1.023 (the bucket is full at the start, so early refill is wasted: the twelfth request needs nine refilled tokens in the 8.8 s after the bucket was last full); and with four at the same moment, no rate works: four at once can't pass a bucket of three. The reader discovers the rule of the whole piece (capacity caps the burst) on their own. Both numbers are checked in demos/token-bucket/test/claims.test.mjs.

“Use interactivity only when interactivity works best.”

~0:13. Case's workshop list (blog.ncase.me/explorable-explanations) calls it “Do, Show, Tell”: do (interact), show (pictures, animation), tell (text). The failure he names is over-interactivity: trying to explain everything with sliders. A definition behind a slider is slower than a sentence.

Nicky Case, on playtesting Earth: A Primer

Gates: withhold the next part

  • Chaim Gingold's Earth: A Primer doesn't let the reader move on until they've done the page's task.
  • Case reports that playtesting is what led there: the next page opens only after the current one is done.
  • In the token bucket (scroll version), the answer and everything after it stay hidden until the reader guesses.

“By withholding an explanation, the explorable explanation can be more effective.”

~0:15. A gate is the opposite of the web's default, where everything is one scroll away. Use it sparingly: one gate at the question that the rest depends on. Always give a way past it (the token bucket has “Skip”), or the reader who already knows leaves.

Amit Patel · Red Blob Games

The explorable is not the explanation

  • The page reads correctly with no interaction at all.
  • Alternate explanation and interaction: a paragraph, a figure, a paragraph.
  • “Guide reader through main concepts, not throw them into an open ended simulation.”
  • Use a simplified version of the problem first, so readers can tell whether this is their problem.

~0:16. Patel has written interactive explanations of game algorithms (pathfinding, hexagonal grids, noise) for over a decade; his talk notes (redblobgames.com/x/2306-educ432) are on the reading list. His ranking of what interaction is for: first helping someone with a task, second understanding, third entertainment. Open the A* page and scroll it without touching anything: it still teaches.

The other side

Most readers never touch it

  • Newsrooms report that only a small share of readers use the controls: Patel cites 10–15% at The New York Times.
  • Readers test the author's implementation, not the topic.

“One doesn’t walk away from the article with the dynamic environment in one’s pocket.”

Andy Matuschak · Most dynamic representations developed for communication aren't very enabling

~0:18. Take the counter-argument seriously. Matuschak's point: an explorable serves the author's argument; when the article ends, the reader has no tool to think with. Patel's: readers fiddle without learning, and they explore his data structures rather than their own. The design response is the previous slide: write for the reader who never clicks, and make the first interaction so cheap that more of them do.

~0:48. Map the board's answers onto the five. Bostock's “Visualizing Algorithms” puts a biased and an unbiased shuffle side by side on the same input, and the bias is visible in one glance. Predict-then-reveal works because a wrong prediction is a surprise you own.

Order of controls · Case's “start small, build big”

One new control per step

  • Teach each control alone; combine them only once each is understood.
  • Ciechanowski's articles add one slider per section, and each figure has only the sliders its paragraph talks about.
  • The token bucket: time first, then the refill rate, then capacity, then editing the traffic.

~0:49. Case's example is the game Miegakure, which teaches each of its mechanics alone before combining them. A new control is a new variable in the reader's head. Two at once and they can't tell which one did what.

Live · demos/token-bucket/02-inputs

Change one view, the others follow

~0:50. Drag the time slider: the bucket, the cursor on the timeline and the sentence all change. Drag a request on the timeline: the outcomes and the token line change. Four views of one state: two figures, a sentence, and the sliders themselves. The sentence is the view that works for a screen reader and for the reader who never clicks. Defaults

The starting values are part of the argument

  • Choose defaults so the first touch shows the idea: the burst of six starts one slider-drag away from the cut-off.
  • A reset returns to them. Each step of a stepper restores its own.
  • Let the reader be wrong cheaply: predict first; reset to the defaults at any time.

~0:52. With a bucket of 10 the six-request burst would all pass and the first figure would show nothing. Default values are the most underrated design decision in an explorable: the reader's first impression is your defaults.

Failures you've already seen

Six ways an explorable fails

  • A dashboard of sliders and no starting point.
  • Animation the reader can't pause or step.
  • Randomness without a seed: the point appears only on some loads.
  • The text and its figure scroll apart.
  • The interesting part is behind three clicks.
  • Information only on hover: invisible on touch screens, and it fires while scrolling.

~0:53. Ask which of these the room met in the gallery. Most have a code fix in Act 4: a stepper, a play/pause loop, a seeded generator, position: sticky, defaults. The last one is a design fix: visible labels and a sentence instead of tooltips.

There is no single correct format

The experts disagree, so test

  • The Pudding advises against steppers: a tap for every step. Scroll instead.
  • Amit Patel prefers sliders to animations: the reader controls time.
  • Nicky Case gates content: the reader can't skip ahead.

Pick per idea, then watch someone use it.

~0:55. The Pudding's article is Russell Samora's “Responsive scrollytelling best practices” (pudding.cool/process/responsive-scrollytelling, 2017). The disagreements come from different audiences: news readers on phones, programmers on desktops, students in a course. The only reliable check is a playtest, which Exercise 3 ends with.

Accessibility

An explorable for everyone who reads it

  • A sentence that states what the figure shows, updated with it.
  • Every control works from the keyboard: each request is a focusable button; arrows move it, Delete removes it.
  • Not colour alone: the refused requests are red and crossed.
  • Announced when the reader stops, not on every tick. Motion that pauses, and starts paused under prefers-reduced-motion.

~0:56. Each item is in the token-bucket demo: renderSentence(); edit.js (pointer and keyboard share one module); the ✕ on refused requests; announce(), which waits 700 ms after the last change before updating an aria-live region, so a slider drag is one announcement, not forty; the play loop in step 07. The sentence also serves the majority who never touch anything.

~1:37. Design · feedback

Output beside the code, errors in words

  • Run on input, after a pause in typing (300 ms), not on every keystroke.
  • Show the console under the code; show the error with its line number.
  • Code that doesn't parse prints nothing: keep the last good output, greyed.
  • A Reset to the starting code, and a link that reproduces exactly this code.

~1:39. Mid-edit, code is broken most of the time. If the output disappears every time a bracket is missing, the reader loses the thing they were comparing against. Reset makes experiments cheap: the reader can break anything and get back. The share link is the code itself, base64url-encoded UTF-8 in the URL's hash (never sent to the server); btoa alone throws on any character outside Latin-1, so encode with TextEncoder first. The TypeScript and Svelte playgrounds do the same, compressed.

Josh Comeau · on CodeMirror vs Monaco

“Most web-based playgrounds are meant to demonstrate a concept, not serve as a daily-driver editor.”

  • A <textarea> with a line-number gutter is often enough.
  • Tab should indent, and Esc should give Tab back to the page: an editor must not trap the keyboard.
  • Challenges with a visible check, and the solution behind <details>.

~1:41. Sandpack ships CodeMirror; Comeau explains why it suits teaching better than Monaco (VS Code's editor, several megabytes, built for writing programs). A teaching playground needs to show a concept and load fast. The keyboard trap is real: a textarea that captures Tab with no way out fails WCAG 2.1.2. The checks in our demo are regular expressions over the output.

demos/playground/playground.js · (L12)

Run the code in a fresh sandboxed frame

  • The reader's code in its own <script>: a syntax error doesn't stop the relay.
  • A run number in every message: late output from a replaced run is ignored.
  • No done within 1.5 s: the frame is removed, and the console says why.

The </script escape matters: without it, a string containing </script> in the reader's code ends the script element early. __show prints values the way a reader writes them (Map(1) {"a" => 1}, undefined, Date(…)): Victor's "show the data" for the console. Quick check · (L12)

Why not just eval(code.value) in the page?

The reader's code would run with the page's rights: its DOM, its cookies, its localStorage. And the code travels in links, so anyone could send a link that runs code on whoever opens it.
A sandboxed frame without allow-same-origin gets an opaque origin: it can't read the page. It can only postMessage, and the page checks event.source === frame.contentWindow.
An infinite loop may still hang the tab, depending on whether the browser gives the frame its own process. The watchdog throws a stuck frame away; a Worker can be terminated outright.

~1:46. Every serious playground runs code in an iframe, usually on a separate origin; Comeau self-hosts his bundler on its own domain. With eval, the share-link feature is an XSS by design. Chrome currently runs this sandboxed frame out of process, so the page stays responsive while the frame spins; not every browser does. Demonstrate with while (true) {} in the playground.

Step 01 · model.js · test/claims.test.mjs

The model knows nothing about the page

  • Parameters in, results out. No DOM, no globals: the figure can be redrawn from scratch any time.
  • It runs in Node, so the explanation's claims are tests: node --test test/claims.test.mjs.

~1:48. A pure model makes every later step cheap: steps, seeds, URLs and time are all ways of choosing the arguments. The test file checks every number in the text, from “four of the six” to “1.05 tokens per second”, plus the outline and the one-new-control rule. The first draft of this demo had a sentence the simulation contradicted (“the bucket holds four”, while it held three at that moment); a test caught it. Wrong numbers in an explanation are worse than none: the reader trusts the figure. The 1 - 1e-9 is a float tolerance: at 0.5 tokens per second the request at 3 s finds exactly one token in real arithmetic, and 1.0000000000000004 in floating point. It passes by a rounding margin of 4×10⁻¹⁶, and rounding could as easily have landed just below 1, giving a refusal the text doesn't explain. The tolerance makes the model's answer the arithmetic's answer.

Step 01 · main.js

One state, three render functions

  • Lecture 5's render(state), with three outputs.
  • Linked views come for free: they read the same state.
  • The sentence is a view too.

~1:50. Ask: what do you need to add a fourth view, say a table of requests? One function, one call. Nothing else changes. The SVG is rebuilt on each render; at this size that's well under a millisecond.

Step 02 · edit.js

Pointer and keyboard, one state

  • setPointerCapture: moves keep arriving when the pointer leaves the SVG. touch-action: none, or the drag scrolls the page.
  • Every drag has a keyboard path; after a re-render, focus returns to the request, or after Delete to the next one in time.

~1:51. Pointer Events (Lecture 11) cover mouse, pen and touch. Pixels become seconds through getScreenCTM().inverse(), so it works at any size. A click without movement removes a request; a four-pixel threshold tells a click from a drag. The first version picked the request by e.target: in a tight burst the enlarged hit circles overlap, and a click on one request removed its neighbour. requestAt() picks the nearest request in time instead, with a reach of at least 22 screen pixels. The marks are drawn in time order, so Tab moves left to right.

Step 03 · steps.js

The explanation is an array

  • Each step sets the author's defaults and lists the controls; the figure is a function of state and step, so Back works.
  • The first words, in order, are the outline: Four · But · Therefore · But · Therefore. A test keeps them that way.

~1:53. Open 03-stepper and click through. The predict step hides the outcomes and the token line, since either would give the answer away. Each step adds at most one control (the test checks that too). Editing steps.js rewrites the explanation without touching any drawing code, which is what you want when a playtest shows a step in the wrong place. The step text is a live region, so it's written only when the step changes, not on every render.

Step 05 · rng.js

The same surprise for every reader

  • Math.random can't be seeded: the burst you wrote about may not happen.
  • Seed 7 always has a burst between 4.65 s and 5.25 s.
  • Not for security; for reproducible demos and tests.

~1:55. Click "Unseeded" a few times in 05, then go back to seed 7. Two of seed 7's requests land at exactly 4.65 s, so they draw as one mark: drag the top one aside to reveal the other (or Tab to both). The text can now refer to "the burst at 4.65 s" and be right for every reader; the test file checks it. mulberry32 is a small public-domain generator with a 32-bit state; good enough for demos, not for cryptography (use crypto.getRandomValues).

Step 06 · url.js

A link opens the figure in a given state

  • The address bar is one more view of the state.
  • The query string comes from strangers: validate and clamp every value.
  • Debounce the writes: Safari throttles replaceState.
  • Links push history, so Back returns to the previous state.

~1:57. With state in the URL, the text can point at the figure ("here is the burst"), a reader can send a teacher exactly what confuses them, and a playtester's state can be reproduced. Try ?cap=99&rate=abc live: it opens at capacity 8 and rate 1.

  • position: sticky keeps the figure in view.

  • A requestAnimationFrame loop that advances by real elapsed time.
  • Play, pause, step; starts paused under prefers-reduced-motion, or when a link asks for a moment (?t=5.3).

Exercise 3 · last 7 minutes

The silent test

  • Your neighbour uses your page. You watch. Don't speak, don't point, don't touch the mouse.
  • Write three things: where they hesitated · did they reach the surprise? · the sentence you wanted to say.
  • Then swap.

~2:33. Swap after three and a half minutes. Before the timer starts, name the three volunteers for Show three. Nothing else for them yet: their laptops are in their testers' hands. PostHog · 24 tips for giving S-tier demos

A live demo follows the same rules

  • One point. Decide the sentence the room should remember. Get to it in the first minute.
  • The pain first. Open with a problem the room already has, not with the feature.
  • Before beside after. The same input through the old way and the new one.
  • How it works, not how you built it. The code walkthrough is a different talk.

~2:40. While this slide and the next are up (to 2:45), the three volunteers copy their folder (or URL) to the projector laptop; only the first plugs in if there's one cable. The question / touch / surprise / rule shape works for a three-minute demo too: the pain is the question, the before-and-after is the surprise. PostHog's list (posthog.com/newsletter/how-to-demo) is written for hackathon demos and holds for any room.

Before you start

Prepare the run, then don't apologise

  • Realistic data, and a seeded or saved state: the surprise happens on cue.
  • Notifications off. Browser zoomed so the back row can read it.
  • The URL bookmarked or in the address bar; the state in the URL.
  • A screenshot of every step, in case the network or the code fails.
  • Rehearse it aloud once.
  • No “sorry, it's rough” before you start. End with a sentence, then stop.

~2:43. Everything from Act 4 pays off here: a seed and a URL make a live demo repeatable. “Sorry, it's still rough” lowers expectations before anyone has seen anything; let the demo speak.
